## Findings

**1. The NATO lesson lifecycle makes "learned" a protected status that only a human authority can grant.** The NATO Lessons Learned Handbook (JALLC) splits the loop into analysis → endorsement and tasking → implementation → validation: leadership must endorse each Lesson Identified, approve or modify its remedial action, and formally task an Action Body to implement it. The Tasking Authority changes the status from Lesson Identified to Lesson Learned only after the remedial action is implemented *and validated*. The state machine itself is the human gate: nothing self-promotes to doctrine. Source: https://www.jallc.nato.int/application/files/3116/0261/5919/Lessons_Learned_Handbook_3rd_Edition.pdf

**2. The Army defines a lesson learned as changed behavior, not a written report.** AR 11-33 (Army Lessons Learned Program) defines a lesson learned as validated knowledge that *leads to a change in behavior*; until then it is only an observation or insight. Observations with DOTMLPF implications go to deputy chiefs of staff and directors, who direct action and monitor disposition, so the decision to change doctrine, organization, or training always passes through a named senior human. Source: http://asktop.net/wp/download/3/r11_33.pdf

**3. The AAR is a collection ritual with a built-in integration chapter most imitators skip.** TC 25-20 structures every AAR around what was supposed to happen, what happened, why, and what to sustain or improve, and insists an AAR "is not a critique" and "does not grade success or failure", which keeps juniors talking. Its Chapter 5 ("Following Up: Using the Results of the After-Action Review") orders the loop closed on the spot: identify tasks requiring retraining, "fix the problem: retrain immediately, revise standard operating procedures". The lesson lands in the unit's own SOPs and next training plan, not in a distant archive. Source: https://nick.groenen.me/attachments/public/gitignored/TC%2025-20%20A%20Leader's%20Guide%20to%20After-Action%20Reviews.pdf

**4. CALL works because it is a standing analysis organization, not a database.** Founded 1 August 1985 at Fort Leavenworth after post-Grenada studies, CALL runs a deliberate annual collection plan, performs root-cause and trend analysis, initiates products (bulletins, handbooks, newsletters), and pushes issues to TRADOC proponents for DOTMLPF resolution; the loop is collect → analyze → disseminate → *integrate* → archive, with integrate named as a distinct step. The DoD-wide repository behind it is JLLIS, whose governing instruction (CJCSI 3150.25) defines a discovery phase and then a validation phase in which designated analysts review each observation for accuracy and relevance before it can progress. Sources: https://www.army.mil/call ; https://www.jcs.mil/Portals/36/Documents/Doctrine/jlld/cjcsi3150_25.pdf

**5. Army doctrine admits only validated lessons, and different layers change at different speeds.** ADP 1-01 (Doctrine Primer) states doctrine "incorporates or adds best practices and validated lessons learned to doctrinal TTP" and that local procedures enter doctrine only after gaining acceptance "over time in varying circumstances"; concepts are explicitly not doctrine until tested and validated. It names two drivers of change with different urgency: threat-driven change demands immediate revision, while deliberate improvement runs on the slow proponent-and-approval cycle; principles (ADPs) move slowly, techniques (ATPs) move fast. This is version discipline: an enduring core with a high-churn periphery, and a human proponent gating promotion between them. Source: https://armypubs.army.mil/epubs/DR_pubs/DR_a/pdf/web/ARN18138_ADP%201-01%20FINAL%20WEB.pdf

**6. ASRS solved the incentive problem with a neutral third party and paid-for immunity.** NASA, not the regulator FAA, receives reports; analysts de-identify them, and the FAA grants limited enforcement immunity (usable once per five years) for violations disclosed via ASRS, which is why the system has collected over 900,000 reports without ever breaching a reporter's identity. Output is push, not just pull: over 5,500 alert bulletins and FYI notices route specific hazards to the organizations that own them, while the CALLBACK newsletter and a public searchable database handle ambient dissemination. Collection volume is bought with blamelessness; dissemination works because alerts are addressed, not broadcast. Sources: https://www.faa.gov/newsroom/aviation-voluntary-reporting-programs-1 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviation_Safety_Reporting_System

**7. CAST closes the loop all the way to outcome measurement, which is why it can claim an 83% result.** The Commercial Aviation Safety Team (regulators, manufacturers, operators, unions at one table, deciding by consensus) runs an explicit chain: analyze data → identify hazards → develop specific safety enhancements → implement → *track implementation* → *monitor effectiveness of the mitigations* → feed experience back into the process. Roughly 100 adopted safety enhancements contributed to an 83% reduction in US commercial fatality risk from 1998 to 2008, and CAST then shifted from forensic accident analysis to prognostic risk detection using ASIAS incident data. Tracking implementation and measuring effect are separate steps; CAST does both. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Aviation_Safety_Team

**8. The NTSB shows what dissemination without integration authority looks like, and how status taxonomy partially compensates.** The Board can only recommend; recipients must respond within 90 days by statute (49 U.S.C. § 1135) but face no implementation deadline, and every recommendation is tracked through a public four-state taxonomy: Open/Closed crossed with Acceptable/Unacceptable. The Most Wanted List is a curated pressure instrument for stale high-value items, yet a 2019 GAO review found the FAA had not fully implemented 35% of aviation recommendations open more than ten years. Persistent, public, per-recommendation state tracking is the compensation for having no enforcement power. Source: https://ntsbauthority.com/ntsb-safety-recommendations

**9. Google SRE treats an unreviewed postmortem as nonexistent and pays people to write good ones.** Postmortem triggers are pre-agreed (user-visible downtime thresholds, any data loss, monitoring failure), drafts pass a senior-engineer review gate for completeness and action-plan quality, and only then are they shared broadly; the chapter is blunt that unreviewed postmortems "might as well never have existed". Dissemination is active ritual, not filing: a monthly postmortem newsletter, reading clubs, and the "Wheel of Misfortune" in which new SREs re-enact old incidents, which doubles as verification that old lessons still live in current heads. Incentives are explicit: peer bonuses, leadership praise at company all-hands, and founder-level participation. Source: https://sre.google/sre-book/postmortem-culture/

**10. The error budget is a pre-agreed decision rule that removes per-case renegotiation.** An error budget is 100% minus the SLO; while budget remains, product teams launch freely, and when it is exhausted, releases freeze and effort shifts to reliability, a rule agreed in advance with executive endorsement so neither side relitigates each incident. This is bounded self-regulation: the system adjusts its own behavior continuously inside limits a human authority set once, and changing the limit (the SLO) requires going back through that authority. Source: https://sre.google/sre-book/embracing-risk/

**11. Toyota kata makes improvement a daily practiced routine with a coach, not an episodic event.** The improvement kata cycles four steps (understand the challenge/direction, grasp the current condition, set the next target condition, experiment toward it via PDCA), and the coaching kata's five questions force each learner-coach pair to check yesterday's experiment before planning the next, so verification of the last change is structurally built into starting the next one. Management's gate is the challenge and target condition; the experiments below it run freely. Rother's point is that improvement fails when practiced as occasional workshops instead of a daily default. Source: https://www.lean.org/lexicon-terms/kata/

**12. Medicine's M&M conference is the cautionary tale: a century-old loop that documents its own failure to close.** Running since Codman's outcome-tracking around 1900, M&M reliably produces discussion but not change: "the case is discussed, the learning points are highlighted, and then the next presentation begins", with no follow-up mechanism and a lingering blame-and-shame culture that suppresses reporting. The evidence-based fixes mirror the strong systems above: a skilled moderator, standardized presentation format, systems-level (not individual) focus, and a dedicated quality committee that tracks each action item to closure and reports back at a later conference. The NRC's generic communications program shows the mature alternative in tiers: Bulletins require a written response under oath with a copy to the resident inspector, Generic Letters require a response, and Information Notices require only review for applicability, so the dissemination tier itself encodes the integration obligation. Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10023258/ ; https://www.nrc.gov/about-nrc/regulatory/gencomms

## The three reasons these loops die, ranked, with the countermeasure

1. **Dissemination without integration (lessons observed, not learned).** The report exists, the behavior never changes: NTSB's 35% of decade-old recommendations unimplemented, M&M's "next presentation begins", the Army's need to legislate that a lesson is not learned until behavior changes. Countermeasure: a lesson state machine with a tasked owner per lesson and a validation step before anything may be marked learned (NATO's endorsement-tasking-validation chain; CAST's separate track-implementation and monitor-effectiveness steps). For tacsop: give every lesson a status, an owner artifact in the graph, and close it only when the downstream adoption gate *and* a behavior check pass.

2. **Incentive failure at collection.** People stop reporting when reports are punished, ignored, or cost unpaid time; GAO found NASA managers skipped LLIS out of "lack of time" and "a perception of intolerance for mistakes" (https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-02-195), and both ASRS and SRE exist as engineered answers (neutral recipient, immunity, blamelessness, peer bonuses, founder attention). Countermeasure: make reporting non-punitive and visibly rewarded, and keep the audit trail evidentiary rather than punitive. For tacsop: keep the shift-left hook non-blocking, and have the review gate praise good session docs the way SRE celebrates good postmortems.

3. **Retrieval failure: the corpus outgrows search and users route around it.** GAO's NASA finding is the canonical statement: lessons "cover so many topics that it is difficult to search for an applicable lesson", so managers used hallway conversations instead; JLLIS accumulates observations faster than validation clears them. Countermeasure: the successful systems stopped relying on pull-search and built push-routing: ASRS alert bulletins and NRC notices go *to the party that owns the hazard*, CALL hands products to units about to deploy. For tacsop this is the direct justification for the knowledge-graph traversal layer: its job is not to make lessons findable but to route each lesson to the doctrine artifact it should update, turning the database from a place lessons rest into a switchboard they pass through.
